Lactate as Fuel and vLa max

Source and boundary. Teaching content below is condensed from ABCC_Level3_Module_1_FINAL.docx (August 2026 manuscript). The 28 September database backup contains private LearnDash Lesson 1.3 (post 11077, course 9059); its title and six distinctive phrases match the manuscript. A full editorial comparison is still needed. This page shows teaching content only — it is not an assessment, and no quiz material is included.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, the candidate will be able to:

  1. Explain the lactate shuttle theory and describe lactate's roles as fuel, signalling molecule and inter-organ substrate carrier.
  2. Define vLa max (maximal lactate accumulation rate) and locate it as a third axis of performance profile alongside VO₂max and CP.
  3. Describe the field test that estimates vLa max from a 15-second maximal sprint, and identify typical value ranges that distinguish sprint-profile from endurance-profile riders.
  4. Read a vLa max value alongside CP/W′ data (from Module 3a) to inform training-fit conversations.

Lactate is not a waste product

Lactate has been misread for a century. The traditional view — lactate as the by-product that causes muscle burn and the bonk — has been overturned by the modern lactate-shuttle picture. Lactate is a fuel moved between cells, between tissues and into mitochondria; a signal driving mitochondrial biogenesis and metabolic adaptation; and the rate at which an athlete produces lactate at maximal glycolytic effort is a measurable, individual, trainable variable.

The foundational modern reference is Brooks (2018), whose Cell Metabolism synthesis remains the standard text for the coach reading lactate physiology. Three shuttles matter:

  • Cell-to-cell. Glycolytic muscle fibres (Type II) produce lactate; oxidative fibres (Type I) and the heart take it up via monocarboxylate transporters (MCT1) and burn it.
  • Cell-to-mitochondrion. Within a single muscle cell, lactate produced in the cytosol is transported into the mitochondrion, converted back to pyruvate, and oxidised. The mitochondrion uses lactate as a preferred substrate in well-trained tissue.
  • Tissue-to-tissue. Liver uptake of lactate produces glucose (Cori cycle). The brain uses lactate as substrate during exercise. The heart relies on lactate as fuel under load.

The implication: lactate accumulation in the blood is not the system failing. It reflects the production–clearance balance. Adaptation raises clearance capacity through increased mitochondrial density, MCT1 expression and oxidative enzymes, so that at any given glycolytic rate blood lactate is lower. Endurance training and hypoxic training both upregulate MCT4 expression.

Lactate as signal

The same molecule that fuels working tissue also signals adaptation. Lactate activates pathways that drive PGC-1α expression, mitochondrial biogenesis and angiogenesis. Repeated exposure to elevated lactate is part of why high-intensity intervals are effective: the lactate is part of the message.

This reframes coaching conversations. Riders who train hard enough to produce significant lactate, and recover well enough to clear it, are signalling adaptation. Riders who only train at very low intensities miss the lactate-driven signalling axis entirely. Riders who chronically sit above threshold accumulate more signal than the system can process and slip toward non-functional overreaching.

vLa max — the third axis

vLa max describes how fast an athlete's glycolytic system can produce lactate when fully activated. Introduced by Mader and colleagues in the 1980s and refined repeatedly, it is independent of VO₂max and of CP. Two riders sharing a CP can show very different vLa max values; the higher-vLa-max rider recruits glycolysis faster and recovers anaerobic capacity differently.

The field-test protocol is a maximal 15-second sprint with blood lactate sampled before and at intervals after. The rate of rise, corrected for the alactic phase, yields an estimate. Harnish and colleagues (2023) reported acceptable reliability when the protocol is controlled; a 2024 follow-up showed that small changes in the alactic-correction method shift the estimate meaningfully; Meixner and colleagues (2024) independently confirmed reliability; Yang and colleagues (2023) refined the calculation using energy-system contributions.

Typical value ranges:

  • vLa max above ~0.7 mmol·L⁻¹·s⁻¹: sprinter, track or short-track profile. Strong glycolytic recruitment, fast anaerobic capacity, less suited to ultra-aerobic events.
  • vLa max below ~0.4 mmol·L⁻¹·s⁻¹: long-duration endurance specialist. Limited glycolytic ceiling, well-suited to GC and TT-type efforts, will struggle in repeated short attacks.
Cross-reference to Module 3a. Module 3a Lesson 3a.3 owns the CP / W′ / FTP test protocols and Module 3a Lesson 3a.4 owns the interval prescription built on this physiology. This lesson teaches what is being measured; Module 3a teaches how to measure it and how to use the numbers.

Practical coaching applications

  • Diagnose the rider, not the discipline. A road racer with surprisingly poor short-effort recovery may have a higher vLa max than expected for the event; a time-trialist who blows up on the first attack may sit on the wrong side of their own glycolytic profile.
  • Match the training to the trait. Riders with high vLa max benefit from work that shifts the glycolytic profile downward — sustained Zone 2 / FAT max sessions (see Lesson 1.2), longer threshold work, fewer ultra-short maximal repeats. Riders with low vLa max chasing criterium results benefit from short maximal sprints and explosive repeats.
  • Calibrate event-fit advice. vLa max gives the coach a vocabulary for honest event-fit conversations. The athlete pursuing a target that does not match their glycolytic profile is choosing the harder path — defensible if they want to, but the coach should make the trade-off explicit.

Key takeaways

  • Lactate is fuel, signal and substrate carrier — not waste.
  • The lactate shuttle moves lactate cell-to-cell, cell-to-mitochondrion and tissue-to-tissue.
  • vLa max is a third performance-profile axis alongside VO₂max and CP, measurable from a 15-second maximal sprint.
  • Sprinter profiles sit above ~0.7 mmol·L⁻¹·s⁻¹; long-duration endurance specialists sit below ~0.4.
  • Module 3a owns the CP/W′ test protocols and interval prescription; this lesson owns the underlying physiology.
