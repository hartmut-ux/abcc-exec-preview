Article 07 · Research review

The Heavy Lift Returns: Why Cyclists Need Strength Training

Byline: to be confirmed before publication Type: Research review

Heavy resistance training is now common in elite road cycling, yet many amateur riders still fear that it will make them heavier and slower. The evidence supports a more useful question: what dose improves cycling performance without displacing the riding that matters?

This article reviews the evidence, outlines a practical annual structure and examines the concern about unwanted mass. Article 09 considers concurrent training, while Article 10 covers bone health.

The evidence base

Heavy resistance training improves cycling performance through three primary mechanisms: improved neuromuscular efficiency (more force from the same muscle), delayed recruitment of less-efficient Type IIx fibres at submaximal intensities (better cycling economy), and increased peak sprint power.

The most informative single body of work comes from Rønnestad and colleagues. The 2022 case report tracking multiple seasons of heavy strength training in highly trained cyclists provided a longitudinal view of how heavy lifting interacts with cycling performance across years rather than weeks (Rønnestad et al., 2022). The pattern: heavy strength maintained or improved key markers — peak power, cycling economy, time-to-exhaustion — across consecutive competitive seasons, without interference with the endurance side.

Kristoffersen and colleagues (2019) compared short-sprint training and heavy strength training in cyclists and found both modalities produced gains, with heavy strength producing larger increases in maximal strength markers and short-sprint training producing slightly larger peak-power changes. Neither approach is universally superior; both have a place, and a well-designed annual plan uses each in its appropriate phase.

A useful and sometimes overlooked finding from Vicari and colleagues (2026) examined the distinction between gym-based strength training and cycling-specific on-bike strength work. Both produce adaptations; the modalities are complementary, not interchangeable, and the gym-based work generates larger neuromuscular changes than the on-bike work alone.

“Neither approach is universally superior; both have a place, and a well-designed annual plan uses each in its appropriate phase.”

A periodised programme structure

Heavy resistance training for cyclists is not a single block. It is a year-long programme with three phases.

Heavy phase (8–12 weeks, late off-season into early base). Loads at 80–90% of one-repetition maximum (1RM); three to five sets of three to six repetitions; two sessions per week separated by at least 48 hours. Compound multi-joint movements dominate: back squat, trap-bar deadlift, Romanian deadlift, single-leg squat variants, hip thrust.

Power phase (4–6 weeks, build phase). Loads at 60–75% 1RM moved with maximal intent, adding explosive variants. The objective shifts from absolute force production to rate of force development.

Maintenance phase (competitive season). One session per week, two to three sets of three to five repetitions at 80–85% 1RM, completed 48–72 hours before key endurance sessions or races — retaining neuromuscular adaptations without adding fatigue.

The “bulky and slow” question

The most common athlete-side resistance to heavy lifting is the fear of hypertrophy. The honest answer: meaningful hypertrophy requires bodybuilder-level training volume combined with bodybuilder-level nutrition. The strength programme described above — moderate volume, no caloric surplus, no protein-stacking intent — does not produce that outcome. Cyclists who report feeling “heavy and slow” after starting strength training may be experiencing novelty, residual soreness, poorly placed sessions or excessive volume; the response is to review the programme, fuelling and recovery.

Three decisions for the coach

  1. Programme by phase, not by month. The heavy phase belongs in the off-season; the power phase bridges into base and build; maintenance carries the work through the season. Skipping the heavy phase sacrifices the foundation; skipping maintenance loses gains within four to six weeks.
  2. Refer for technique, programme for outcome. Coaches without specific strength qualifications should refer technique coaching to a qualified strength practitioner, then own the programme — loads, repetitions and schedule, integrated with the cycling plan.
  3. Use enough volume, not the most volume. Moderate volume can produce cycling-relevant outcomes without unnecessary fatigue; progress from the rider's training history, response and place in the season.
